The call you worked eighteen months for lasts about twenty minutes, and most families spend it answering questions instead of asking them.

That is a missed opportunity twice over. You lose the information you need to compare programs, and you miss the chance to show a coach that your athlete is serious and thinking clearly. Coaches notice good questions. They are a signal all by themselves.

Your athlete should ask most of these. You can ask the money ones.

## The short list: 10 questions to ask college coaches

Bring these to every first call. The sections below explain what each answer tells you.

1. Where do you see me fitting in your squad?
2. How many players are you recruiting at my position for my class?
3. What would my first year realistically look like?
4. How would you describe your style of play?
5. What does a typical week look like in season and out?
6. What happens if I get injured?
7. How do you handle conflicts between training and classes or labs?
8. What do your players major in?
9. What would a realistic offer look like for me?
10. What would you like to see from me next?

## About the roster and playing time

**Where do you see me fitting in your squad?** The single most useful question. Listen for a specific position and a specific year. Vague answers usually mean vague interest.

**How many players are you recruiting in my position for my class?** If they are taking three keepers, that changes what the offer means.

**What does the depth chart look like at my position right now?** Who is ahead, and what year are they.

**What would my first year realistically look like?** A coach who says you will start immediately is selling. A coach who describes a competitive path is being honest.

## About the program

**How would you describe your style of play?** Then ask a follow-up about how their athlete's strengths fit it. This is the question that proves your kid researched them.

**What does a typical week look like in season and out?** Hours, travel, training load. This is the four-year reality.

**What happens if I get injured?** Ask it directly. The answer tells you what kind of program this is. Ask whether the scholarship is affected, who handles rehab, and whether injured players stay part of the group.

**How long do you plan to be here?** Coaches move, and your athlete may be recruited by someone who is gone in two years. Nobody can promise, and the reaction is informative.

## About academics

**How do you handle conflicts between training and labs or classes?** Especially important for nursing, engineering and education majors, where required afternoon sessions collide with practice.

**What academic support exists, and do players use it?**

**What do your players major in?** If nobody in the program studies what your athlete wants to study, find out why.

If the first call goes well, a campus visit usually comes next. Know [the difference between an official and an unofficial visit](/college-soccer-visits/) before you go.

## On the visit: questions to ask the players

The players will tell your athlete things the coach will not. Find a few minutes away from the staff and ask:

**What surprised you most about your first year?**

**How much do freshmen play?** Compare the answer with what the coach told you.

**What is the coach like after a loss?**

**Do you have time for your major, and for anything outside soccer?**

**Would you choose this school again?** Watch the pause before the answer.

## About money, and ask this early

**What would a realistic offer look like for me?** Ask before the visit ends. Families avoid this and then spend months on a school they were never going to afford.

**Is the athletic award renewable, and on what terms?**

**What does the total cost look like after everything?** Athletic aid, academic aid, need-based aid, fees, travel home. Compare final numbers between schools, since a smaller soccer offer at a cheaper school often wins.

**Are there benefits I should know about?** Some families qualify for programs like Yellow Ribbon or military tuition benefits that change the math. Ask, because nobody will volunteer it.

## When an offer comes

**Is this a verbal offer or a written one, and when would we sign?** My post on [verbal commitment vs signing](/verbal-commitment-vs-signing/) explains why the difference matters.

**How long do we have to decide?** A fair program gives a family time. A deadline of a few days is pressure, and it is fine to ask why.

**If the staff changes, what happens to this offer?**

## Two things to do around the call

**Prepare three questions your athlete cares about.** Written down. Nerves erase memory, and a coach can hear the difference between prepared and improvised.

**Stay in the channel the coach used.** If they texted, reply by text. If they emailed, email. Switching channels loses messages and reads as not paying attention.

## And afterwards

Same day, your athlete sends a short thank-you. One thing they took from the conversation, and one clear next step: *what would you like from me next.*

## FAQ

**What are the best questions to ask college coaches?**
Where you fit in the squad, how many they are recruiting at your position, what a typical week looks like, what happens if you get injured, how academics and training conflict, and what a realistic offer would be.

**Should parents talk on the recruiting call?**
Let the athlete lead. Parents are best placed to ask the money and logistics questions near the end.

**When should we ask about scholarship money?**
Early, on the first substantive call or visit. Families who wait spend months on schools they cannot afford.

**What should we ask about injuries?**
Whether the award is affected, who manages rehab, and whether injured players stay involved with the team. The answer reveals a lot about the culture.

**What do we send after the call?**
A same-day thank-you naming one thing from the conversation and asking what the coach would like next.
