A coach invites your athlete to campus and your family spends the drive home replaying every sentence, trying to work out what it meant.

Visits are the clearest signal in recruiting, if you know which kind you were on and what to watch for while you were there.

## The difference

**An unofficial visit** is one your family pays for. Travel, hotel, food, all of it. Your athlete can take as many as they want, at any point, and can do them as early as they like. A visit can be as small as attending a game and saying hello.

**An official visit** is paid for by the school. Travel, lodging, meals for your athlete and usually for parents, over a set period. Programs are limited in how many they can host, and each athlete is limited in how many they can take.

That limit is the whole signal. A program spending one of its official visits on your athlete is spending a scarce resource. Coaches do not do that for players they are unsure about.

In practice, the fully paid version is a Division I thing. Other levels are allowed to host official visits, but most do not have the budget to fly a recruit in and put them up. When Izi was coming back from an ACL tear, UC Riverside flew her out and paid for the flight, the meals and the hotel. A Division I staff spending that on an athlete who was still in rehab is about as clear a signal as recruiting gets.

Rules on when official visits can begin and how many are allowed have changed in the last few years. Ask the coach what applies to your athlete's class rather than relying on what an older sibling went through.

## What to do on an unofficial visit

Treat it as reconnaissance, and go early. Sophomore and junior year unofficial visits cost a tank of gas and tell your family more than any brochure.

Watch a game if you can. See how the coach behaves on the sideline when things go badly, because that is the coach your athlete will play for. Watch how the players on the bench act. A team that is engaged when losing tells you something a highlight video never will.

Walk the campus without the tour. Eat somewhere students eat. If your athlete would be miserable there without soccer, cross it off, because injuries happen and the school has to stand on its own.

Email the coach beforehand. "We will be on campus Saturday for the game against X, and I would love to say hello." Low pressure, and it puts a face to the name in their inbox.

## What an official visit is for

Both directions. The program is deciding about your athlete, and your athlete is deciding about the program. Families forget the second half and spend the whole trip trying to impress.

Your athlete will usually meet the staff, see facilities and academic buildings, sit with an academic advisor, spend time with current players, and often stay with the team.

The most valuable hours are the ones without coaches in the room. Time with current players is where the honest answers live.

## What to ask while you are there

**Ask players, away from staff:** Would you choose this program again? What is the hardest part of being here? How does the coach handle a player in a slump? What happens when someone gets injured?

That last one matters more than families realize. How a program treats injured players tells you what they think of athletes as people. Izi has lived both sides of that.

**Ask the coach:** Where do you see my athlete in the depth chart? What does the recruiting class look like at their position? What does a normal week look like in season and out? What is the plan for their development in the first two years?

**Ask the academic staff:** How do athletes in demanding majors manage travel? What happens if my athlete misses class for a road trip? Can they do a major that conflicts with practice times?

**Ask the financial aid office**, and do this even on a great visit. Get the full cost after all aid in writing. Enthusiasm on a campus tour has convinced many families to skip this conversation and regret it in August.

## Reading the signals

Interest looks like specifics. A coach who has thought about your athlete talks about where they fit, who they would compete with, and what the first two years look like. Vague warmth is a coach being nice.

Watch how much time the head coach spends with your family. On an official visit, a head coach who is in and out is telling you where your athlete sits in their class.

Notice whether they ask about your athlete's other schools. Coaches who want a player want to know who they are competing with.

## After the visit

Your athlete sends a thank-you the same day. Short, specific, and mentioning something real from the trip. Same-day follow-up is one of the things Izi drills, because it is easy and almost nobody does it.

Then write down, that night, what your athlete liked and did not. Visits blur together, and the details that matter in March are the ones nobody wrote down in October.

## FAQ

**What is the difference between an official and unofficial visit?**
The school pays for an official visit and is limited in how many it can host. Your family pays for an unofficial visit, and there is no limit on how many your athlete takes.

**Does an official visit mean an offer is coming?**
Not a guarantee, but it is a strong signal. Official visits are a limited resource, and programs do not spend them on athletes they are unsure about.

**When can soccer recruits take official visits?**
The rules on timing have changed in recent years and vary by division. Ask the coach what applies to your athlete's graduating class.

**What should my athlete ask current players on a visit?**
Whether they would choose the program again, how the coach handles a player in a slump, and what happens when someone gets injured. Ask when staff are not present.

**Should we visit schools before we have coach interest?**
Yes. Unofficial visits cost little, and seeing campuses early makes the target list sharper long before offers appear.
