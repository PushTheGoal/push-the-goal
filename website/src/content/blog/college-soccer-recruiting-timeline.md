"What should we be doing right now?"

That is the question I hear from soccer parents more than any other. Not because they are not working hard. Most families I talk to are doing plenty. They are doing it without a map, and recruiting is one of those journeys where working hard in the wrong order costs you real opportunities.

So here is the map. Grade by grade, this is what the college soccer recruiting timeline looks like, and what your athlete should be focused on at each stage.

## The one date every soccer family should know

Before we go grade by grade, mark this: for NCAA Division I and Division II programs, college coaches cannot directly contact your athlete until **June 15 after their sophomore year**. Before that date, coaches can watch, evaluate, and take notes, but they cannot call, text, or email your player back.

Two things families miss about this rule, and both of them open doors.

**It applies to D1 and D2 only.** Division III, NAIA, and junior college coaches have far more flexibility and can talk with your athlete long before that date. If you have written those levels off, you are ignoring the programs that can build a relationship with your player right now.

**Even D1 and D2 coaches can email your athlete back about their camps.** That reply is about the camp, so read it as a conversation starter rather than a recruiting offer, and treat the camp as a chance to be seen up close.

That single rule explains so much of the confusion families feel. Freshmen and sophomores email coaches, hear nothing, and assume nobody is interested. In many cases the coach saw the email and was not allowed to respond yet. Recruiting rules do get updated, so always verify the current calendar on the NCAA site, but June 15 after sophomore year is the gate to build your plan around.

## The timeline at a glance

| Grade | The focus | Do this |
|---|---|---|
| **Freshman** | Build the foundation | Strong grades, meaningful minutes, record games, start a wide list, send the first emails |
| **Sophomore** | Get organized and on film | First highlight video, recruiting profile, keep emailing, circle June 15 |
| **Junior** | The heavy lifting year | Follow up every week or two, share event schedules, take visits, narrow to 8 to 10 conversations |
| **Senior** | Decide and finish strong | Push conversations to decisions, widen the net if needed, keep sending film |

## Freshman year: build the foundation

Nobody is offering your freshman a scholarship this year, and that is fine. Freshman year is about becoming the kind of player and student coaches want later.

- **Grades come first.** A strong GPA from day one is the cheapest recruiting advantage there is. Coaches pass on talented players with weak transcripts every single season, because those players limit which schools can admit them.
- **Compete at the right level.** Your athlete needs to be playing meaningful minutes against strong competition. That matters more than the badge on the jersey.
- **Start a simple film habit.** Have someone record games now. You do not need a highlight reel yet. You need the habit and the footage.
- **Dream wide, and think bigger than feels reasonable.** Start a list of 50 to 100 schools across Division I, Division II, Division III, NAIA, and junior college. Most families build a list of ten and wonder why nothing happens. The list will change many times. That is the point.
- **Start sending emails this year.** Freshman year is not too early, and the sooner your athlete's name is in front of coaches, the longer those coaches get to watch them grow. D1 and D2 coaches cannot reply yet, and they do read and file what comes in. D3, NAIA, and junior college coaches can write back today. ## Sophomore year: get organized and get on film

This is the year the work starts to look like recruiting.

- **Build the first highlight video.** Three to four minutes of your athlete's best moments. It will not be perfect. It needs to exist, because everything in the next stage depends on it.
- **Create a recruiting profile.** One page with the essentials: graduation year, position, club, GPA, contact info, and the video link.
- **Keep the outreach rolling.** The emails your athlete started sending freshman year continue here, with fresh film and new results each time. Every school on the target list should know their name well before June 15 arrives.
- **Circle June 15 after sophomore year.** When that date hits, the phone can ring. Athletes who did the prep work sometimes hear from programs that same week. Athletes who did nothing hear silence, and it has nothing to do with talent.

## Junior year: the heavy lifting year

If recruiting has a peak season, this is it. Most serious recruiting conversations happen during junior year.

- **Communicate consistently.** Your athlete should be emailing and following up with a focused list of schools, updating coaches with new film, schedules, and results. One email is not a strategy. A rhythm of honest updates is.
- **Get seen in person.** Coaches recruit players they have watched live. Share tournament and showcase schedules with target coaches two to three weeks ahead, with jersey number and field times.
- **Take visits.** Unofficial visits, campus tours, and conversations with coaches tell your family what fits, academically, athletically, and financially.
- **Narrow the list.** The big list from freshman year has done its job. By spring of junior year, it should be down to 8 or 10 real conversations with programs that are answering.

## Senior year: decide and finish strong

For athletes who did the work, senior year is about choosing. For late starters, there is still a path, and speed matters more than polish.

- **Push conversations to decisions.** Ask coaches directly where your athlete stands and what the next step is. Honest answers, even hard ones, are gifts at this stage.
- **Widen the net if needed.** Division III, NAIA, and junior college programs recruit later and hold real opportunities and real money. Plenty of great college careers start there.
- **Keep playing and keep sending film.** Coaches fill roster spots into the spring. The athlete who sends a strong senior-season highlight in January can still land a home.

## After the signing: making the team

Committing gets your athlete onto the roster. Preseason decides where they play. These are the things I tell every athlete heading into a college preseason or a new team's tryout:

- **Get in shape before you arrive.** Ask the coach ahead of preseason which fitness tests they run, then find someone to train with until you pass them. You do not want fitness on your mind on day one.
- **Be a teammate first.** A "great cross" to a teammate or cheering another player's goal tells a coach you care about more than yourself. That makes you more valuable.
- **Run your race.** Do not get caught up in whoever you think is beating you out. Never make an enemy of someone who threatens your spot. Go out of your way to be friends with them, and learn from them.
- **Be helpful.** Ask the coach if there is anything you can do before practice. Pump the balls, carry the gear, check the ball count after.
- **Introduce yourself to the whole staff.** Assistant coaches, the athletic trainer, the facilities manager. Let them all know who you are.
- **Have fun.** Tryouts are stressful, and the whole point of soccer is that it is a game. Athletes who remember that play their best.

## The honest truth about timelines

Families ask me if they are behind. Here is the honest answer: the timeline above is the ideal, and almost nobody runs it perfectly. I did not. What separates athletes who get recruited from athletes who get overlooked is rarely talent. It is having a plan and working it consistently, starting from wherever you are today.

If your athlete is a freshman, you are early. Build the foundation. If your athlete is a junior who has done none of this, you are not doomed. You do not have a year to waste.

## FAQ

**When does college soccer recruiting start?**
Freshman year, and your athlete should be sending emails then. NCAA D1 and D2 coaches cannot directly contact recruits until June 15 after sophomore year, though they can reply about their camps. D3, NAIA, and junior college coaches can respond any time. The smart move is to be organized, on film, and on coaches' radars early.

**What grade is most important for soccer recruiting?**
Junior year carries the most recruiting activity, but sophomore year preparation determines how good your junior year is. Grades matter every year.

**Is senior year too late to get recruited for college soccer?**
No, but the approach changes. Senior-year recruiting means moving fast, contacting programs that recruit late, and staying open to D3, NAIA, and junior college paths. See my full guide on [late recruiting](/is-it-too-late-to-get-recruited/).
