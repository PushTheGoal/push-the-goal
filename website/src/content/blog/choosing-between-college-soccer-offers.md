After two years of chasing interest, having a choice feels like the end of the work. It is the most consequential decision in the whole process and the one families spend the least time on.

Your athlete will live at this school for four years. The soccer might last two of them.

## Compare on paper, not on feeling

Get the offers side by side in a document. Every column filled in for every school before anyone discusses it.

**Net cost to your family per year.** After athletic aid, academic aid, need-based aid, everything. The number your family writes checks for. This belongs in the first column because it is the one that constrains all the others.

**Whether the degree is there.** If the school does not have your athlete's major, it does not matter how good the soccer is. Athletes transfer over this constantly.

**Depth chart reality.** How many players are in that position group, what year are they, who graduates when your athlete arrives.

**The coach's exact words about playing time.** Written down as said, not as remembered.

**Distance, and how often your family would realistically travel.**

**What happens if the soccer ends.** Injury, a coaching change, your athlete deciding at twenty that they are done. Would they still want to be at this school? If the answer is no for one of the offers, that is a substantial mark against it.

Fill it in before the emotional conversation. Families who talk first and fill in the columns after end up justifying a decision they already made.

## The questions to ask before deciding

Ask these of every program still in the running.

**"Where do you see me in the depth chart as a freshman?"** A coach who has thought about your athlete gives a specific answer. Vagueness here is an answer too.

**"How many players are you recruiting in my position in this class?"** If they are taking three of your athlete's position, that changes the picture entirely.

**"What is your plan for my development in the first two years?"** Coaches who develop players can describe how. Coaches who collect players cannot.

**"How many of your players graduate, and how many transfer out?"** Transfer numbers are the tell. A program losing large numbers every year is telling you something the campus visit will not.

**"What happens if I get injured?"** Whether aid is protected, what the medical support looks like, whether injured players stay part of the group. Ask the players this one too, and ask them without staff in the room.

**"Is this offer in writing, and when do I need to decide?"** Get the answer and get the paperwork.

## Red flags

**Pressure to decide immediately.** "This offer is good for forty-eight hours" is a sales tactic. Legitimate deadlines exist and get explained calmly with a reason. Manufactured urgency is a coach who does not want your athlete talking to other programs or thinking too hard.

**Vagueness about money.** A coach who will not put the aid number in writing, or who keeps saying it will work out, is not to be relied on. Get it documented.

**Refusing access to current players.** Any healthy program is happy for a recruit to talk to the team without staff present. Reluctance is loud.

**High transfer numbers.** Look at the roster from two years ago against today. A lot of names missing means athletes are leaving, and athletes leave for reasons.

**Bad-mouthing other programs.** A coach who spends the conversation running down the other schools on your athlete's list is telling you how they talk about people.

**A coaching contract running out.** Ask how long the coach has left. A staff in the final year of a deal may not be there when your athlete arrives.

**Nobody asks about academics.** A coach who never asks what your athlete wants to study, or how school is going, has one interest in them.

**Your athlete goes quiet on campus.** Teenagers know. If your athlete is subdued on a visit and cannot say why, take it seriously rather than talking them out of it.

## Whose decision it is

Your athlete's, with the money as a boundary your family sets.

Parents should be honest and early about what is affordable. "Anything up to this number works, past that we cannot" is fair and it prevents the worst outcome, which is an athlete falling for a school the family cannot fund.

Inside that boundary, let them choose. An athlete who picked their school stays through a hard freshman year. An athlete whose parents picked it transfers, and the reason they give is usually the parents.

## The tiebreaker

When two offers look close, ask the question that cuts through: **would my athlete be happy at this school if they stopped playing tomorrow?**

Soccer careers end. They end with injuries, with coaching changes, with an athlete deciding they want their Saturdays. The school outlasts the sport almost every time.

Pick the place that still works when the soccer stops. That is the one worth committing to.

## FAQ

**How should we compare two college soccer offers?**
Side by side on paper: net cost after all aid, whether the major exists, depth chart at the position, the coach's exact words on playing time, distance, and whether your athlete would want the school without soccer.

**What are red flags in a college soccer program?**
Pressure to decide immediately, vagueness about money, refusing access to current players, high transfer numbers, bad-mouthing other programs, and never asking about academics.

**How much time does my athlete have to decide?**
Ask, and expect a calm explanation. Real deadlines come with reasons. A forty-eight hour ultimatum is a tactic.

**Should parents choose the school?**
Parents set the financial boundary. Inside that, the athlete chooses. Athletes who picked their own school are far more likely to stay through a hard first year.

**What is the best tiebreaker between two similar offers?**
Whether your athlete would still be happy there if they stopped playing tomorrow. The school outlasts the soccer in most careers.
