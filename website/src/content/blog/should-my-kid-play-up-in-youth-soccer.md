Tryouts end, and a coach pulls you aside: "We'd like your daughter to play up." It feels like a compliment, and it usually is. It is also one of the biggest decisions in a young player's soccer life, and it gets made in a parking lot in about ninety seconds.

Playing up can speed up development. It can also put a twelve-year-old on a bench for a season and take the fun out of the game. Here is how to tell which one you are looking at.

## Why playing up helps

**Faster game, faster decisions.** Older players close space quicker. A young athlete who keeps up learns to scan earlier, touch less and decide faster. Those habits carry all the way to college recruiting.

**Bigger challenge.** Some kids coast at their own age group. A tougher environment brings out their best.

**Better coaching.** Sometimes the older team has the stronger coach, and that alone can be worth the move.

## Why playing up can hurt

**Minutes.** The most common way playing up goes wrong. A young athlete who plays every minute with their own age group may play ten minutes a game with the older team. Development happens in minutes. A season on the bench teaches little.

**Confidence.** A kid who was the best player on the field becomes the one who gets knocked off the ball. Some athletes rise to that. Others start playing scared, avoiding the ball and the mistakes that come with it.

**The physical gap.** At these ages, a year can mean a big difference in size and strength. Technique has to carry a younger player, and that is a lot to ask every game.

**Friends and fun.** Kids quit sports they stop enjoying. My post on [why talented kids quit soccer at 14](/why-kids-quit-soccer-at-14/) covers how often that starts in middle school. Leaving their friends for a team of older strangers is a bigger deal to a twelve-year-old than most parents expect.

## The question that matters most

Ask the coach directly: **"How many minutes do you expect her to play?"**

If the answer is real minutes in real games, playing up is worth a serious look. If the answer is vague, or "she'll earn her time," your athlete may spend the season watching. Minutes matter more than the badge on the jersey, at twelve and at seventeen.

Then ask a few more:

- What position will they play, and is it one they want to grow into?
- Can they move back to their own age group if it is not working?
- How does the club decide who plays up and who stays?

## Ask your athlete too

The decision belongs to the family, and your kid's voice should be in it. Some athletes want the challenge and light up when they get it. Others want to stay with their friends and their role. Both are fine at this age. An athlete who chose the move commits to it. An athlete who was moved is more likely to check out.

## Watch the first six weeks

If your athlete plays up, check in after the first month or so:

- Are they getting real minutes?
- Do they look forward to practice, or dread it?
- Are they still trying things on the ball, or only getting rid of it?

If the answers trend the wrong way, it is fine to move back. Nobody recruits a high school player based on which age group they played at twelve. ## FAQ

**Is playing up good for youth soccer players?**
It can be, when the athlete gets real minutes against the older group and still enjoys the game. A season on the bench usually does more harm than good.

**How do I know if my kid is ready to play up?**
Ask the coach how many minutes to expect, watch how your athlete handles the speed and physical gap, and ask your kid whether they want it.

**Does playing up help with college recruiting later?**
Not directly. College coaches recruit high school players and do not look at middle school age groups. Playing up only helps if it makes your athlete a better player.

**Can my kid move back to their own age group?**
Ask before agreeing. Many clubs allow it, and moving back after a rough start is a normal decision.
