Nobody hands parents a job description for this. So most default to one of two settings: run the entire process, or stay out of it completely.

Both cost athletes opportunities. The useful version sits in between, and it has clear lines.

## Start here: they are evaluating you too

Coaches are recruiting a family, and they are honest about it in private. They have all coached a talented athlete whose parent made four years difficult, and they will pass on that situation to avoid repeating it.

That is a reason to understand which parts are yours.

## What belongs to your athlete

**The emails.** Coaches can tell instantly. An email from a parent's address about their child reads differently from an athlete's own, and it raises a question the coach cannot unask: does this kid want it, or does the family?

Help draft them. Sit beside them. Send it from your athlete's address, under their name.

**The phone calls.** If a coach calls, your athlete answers and talks. A seventeen-year-old who can hold a conversation with an adult stands out, because plenty cannot.

**Their social media and their highlight reel.** My division of labor with my clients puts these squarely on the athlete. It is their brand and their film, and doing it teaches them something.

**The final decision.** Inside the financial boundary you set. Athletes who chose their school stay. Athletes whose parents chose transfer, and they tend to say why.

## What belongs to you

**The money.** Entirely yours, and it should be settled early. Tell your athlete plainly what is affordable before they fall for a school that is not. Run the net-cost comparisons. Handle the financial aid forms. Talk to admissions and financial aid offices, which are adult conversations about family finances.

**The logistics.** Travel, registration, schedules, the calendar. Real work, and helpful.

**The tracking.** Recruiting collapses in the follow-up more than in the writing. Keeping the spreadsheet of who was emailed, when, and what came back is a parent's contribution that changes outcomes.

**The accountability.** Your athlete will not want to send follow-up emails in week nine. I build stakes into my coaching for exactly this reason, and in a family it looks like: when are the emails going out, and what happens if they do not.

**The steadiness.** The hardest and most valuable one. Recruiting is months of silence with occasional spikes. Your athlete reads every quiet week as a verdict on their worth. Being the person who knows a coach's inbox in October is not a referendum on your kid is the job.

## The habits that cost athletes

**Talking to coaches instead of letting your athlete talk.** A parent who answers a question directed at their child has told the coach a great deal in one second.

**Sideline behavior.** Coaches watch the parents at showcases. Working the referee for ninety minutes, coaching from the touchline, or visibly reacting to your athlete's mistakes will end interest before anyone speaks to you. Every college coach has a story.

**Leading with what your athlete deserves.** Emails and conversations built around how underrated your child is, or how badly their club has treated them, land as a preview of four years of complaints.

**Criticizing the current coach.** However justified. A coach hears how you will talk about them.

**Making it the only subject.** Recruiting can eat every car ride and dinner in a family. Athletes burn out on the process before they burn out on soccer, and that burnout shows up as an athlete who quietly stops caring.

**Comparing.** To the teammate who committed, to a sibling, to who your athlete was before the injury. Nothing useful comes from it.

## The conversation worth having early

Sit down with your athlete and split it up on paper. What is theirs, what is yours, what is shared.

Then agree when you will talk about recruiting. Some families do Sunday evenings for twenty minutes. Outside that window it is not the topic. That single boundary protects the relationship, and the relationship is going to outlast the recruiting by fifty years.

## The thing to hold onto

Your athlete is going to spend a year being ignored by adults they hoped would want them. That is the ordinary experience of recruiting, not a sign anything is wrong.

What they need at home is not another evaluator. They need someone who is certain about them while the emails go unanswered, and who makes sure the next one still goes out.

The results follow consistency, and consistency at sixteen is mostly a function of who is in the house.

## FAQ

**Should parents email college coaches?**
No. Coaches want emails from the athlete, in their own name. Parents can help draft them, and the message should come from the athlete's address.

**What should parents handle in soccer recruiting?**
Money and net-cost comparisons, travel and logistics, tracking outreach, accountability on follow-ups, and keeping the athlete steady through long quiet stretches.

**Do college coaches evaluate parents?**
Yes, and they are candid about it. Sideline behavior, how you talk about your athlete's current coach, and whether you answer questions meant for your child all register.

**Can a parent talk to a college coach at all?**
Yes, especially about cost, academics and logistics. Let your athlete lead conversations about soccer and about themselves.

**How do we keep recruiting from taking over family life?**
Set a time to discuss it, once a week, and keep it out of the rest of the conversation. Athletes burn out on the process before they burn out on the sport.
