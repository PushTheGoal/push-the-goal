A coach replied. They asked for film. They said they would watch your athlete at a showcase. Your family got excited, and then nothing for six weeks.

This is the part of recruiting that breaks parents. Not rejection, which at least resolves. Silence, which sits there.

## What the silence usually is

Almost never a decision about your athlete. The common reasons:

**They filled the position.** Signed someone else in that class and moved on without telling the other athletes, because sending twenty rejection emails is nobody's favorite afternoon.

**They are waiting.** Holding the spot to see what the [transfer portal](/transfer-portal-high-school-recruits/) offers in spring. Your athlete is a real option they are not ready to commit to.

**Their season started.** In season, a college coach is coaching, traveling, scouting and recruiting all at once. Recruiting email is the thing that slips.

**The email got buried.** Coaches at bigger programs get hundreds a week.

**A staff change.** The assistant handling recruiting left, and the inbox went with them.

Notice that four of those five have nothing to do with your athlete's ability. Families read silence as a verdict. It is usually a logistics problem.

## Follow up more than feels comfortable

The single most common recruiting mistake is stopping after two emails.

Izi's athletes get responses on the third and fourth round. Not the first. Coaches have told her athletes outright that this was the fourth or fifth email, which is exactly the point. Volume of contact over months reads as genuine interest, and genuine interest is what separates your athlete from the hundred form letters in that inbox.

So: follow up every two to three weeks. Not daily, not once a semester. Every couple of weeks, with something new each time.

Izi's biggest rule for a quiet coach: every follow-up adds value, and the most valuable thing your athlete can send is new film. A coach who went quiet on a reel from August has a real reason to open an email with a clip from last weekend.

**What counts as something new.** A new highlight clip. An upcoming event with team, field and times. A result or an award. A grade update. A short question about their program.

**What does not count.** "Just checking in." "Circling back." "Following up on my last email." Those are not messages, they are throat-clearing, and coaches skip them.

## The logistics email, which always works

The best re-entry into a dead thread is information a coach needs rather than a request.

Your athlete has an event coming up. They send the team, the field, the times, the jersey number, and note anything that changed. Izi's version of the line: "Hey coach, I just want to make sure you had eyes on this. I would hate for you to go to the wrong field."

Nothing is being asked for. It is useful. It is impossible to resent. And it puts your athlete back at the top of the inbox with a reason to be there.

Same-day thank-you notes work the same way. If a coach came to a game, your athlete emails that night: thanks for coming, a new reel is on the way, and "if there is anything else you would like to see to continue my recruitment, I would love to know what that is." That question invites a reply and gives your athlete a job if it comes.

## When the normal follow-ups have died

After a couple of dead follow-ups, stop sending the same email again. It is not working and repeating it is not going to start working.

Izi's instruction to athletes at this point is to do something they are not expecting. She has had an athlete send a coach who ghosted them a funny reaction GIF over text. Not a paragraph. A GIF.

It works because it is human, it is low stakes, and it does not read like the two hundred identical follow-ups already sitting unread. Her framing to the athlete: have some personality, do not stress it, there is nothing to lose right now.

That last clause is the whole permission. If the thread is dead, the downside of a bit of personality is zero. The downside of a fifth "circling back" is that it stays dead.

Two rules on this. Stay in the channel the coach used, so if they texted, text. And keep it warm and appropriate. Personality, not gimmick.

## When to stop

Rarely, and not because of silence alone.

Stop when a coach says no. Stop when they sign someone in your athlete's position and the class is closed, which they will tell you if asked directly.

Otherwise keep the program on the list at a lower intensity. Coaches change jobs constantly, and the coach who never answered at one school will be at another school in two years remembering the athlete who kept showing up in their inbox politely for a year. Any coach relationship is worth keeping.

## The part for parents

Your athlete sends these. Not you.

A parent's email about their own child reads differently than an athlete's email about themselves, and coaches notice immediately. Help draft it. Sit next to them. Do not send it from your address.

And when the silence stretches, keep the discouragement off your athlete. They are already reading it as a verdict on them. Your job is to be the person who knows it is a full inbox in October, and who makes sure the next follow-up goes out anyway.

## FAQ

**Why did a college coach stop responding to my athlete?**
Usually because they filled the position, are waiting on the transfer portal, got busy in season, or lost the email. Silence is rarely a decision about your athlete.

**How many times should my athlete follow up with a coach?**
More than most families do. Responses commonly come on the third or fourth round. Follow up every two to three weeks with something new each time.

**What should a follow-up email say?**
Something that adds value, and new film first: a fresh clip, an event with field and times, a result, a grade update. Avoid "just checking in" and "circling back", which coaches skip.

**What if the coach still does not answer?**
Change the approach rather than repeating it. A short, human, unexpected message in the channel the coach used has restarted threads that formal follow-ups could not.

**When should my athlete give up on a program?**
When the coach says no or confirms the class is closed at that position. Otherwise keep them on the list, since coaches move schools and relationships carry over.
