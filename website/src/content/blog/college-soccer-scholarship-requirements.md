There are two sets of requirements in college soccer, and families usually only prepare for one.

The first is athletic: can your kid play at this level. That is the one everybody thinks about. The second is academic and administrative, and it is the one that quietly disqualifies athletes every single year, often after a coach has already offered.

You need both. And at some levels the order flips.

At Division III, academics come first. Same at any Ivy League program. The coach's interest matters, and it will not save an application that admissions will not take. Families aiming at those schools should treat the transcript as the primary recruiting asset and the soccer as the thing that gets them a closer look.

## The athletic bar

Coaches are evaluating four things, in roughly this order.

**Speed of decision.** How fast your athlete reads the game and acts. This is what separates levels more than anything physical.

**Competitive level.** Meaningful minutes against strong opponents. A coach would rather see your athlete tested and imperfect than dominant against nobody.

**Position-specific ability.** What the role demands. A center back's requirements are not a winger's, and film that shows the wrong things reads as a player who does not understand their own position.

**Trajectory.** Coaches recruit who your athlete will be in two years. A sophomore with obvious upside beats a senior who has plateaued.

## The academic requirements nobody plans for

**NCAA Eligibility Center registration.** Athletes competing at NCAA D1 or D2 must register and be certified. This is not automatic and not something a coach does for you. Families who leave it late find out in senior spring that a core course does not count.

**Core course rules.** The NCAA counts specific approved courses, not your athlete's whole transcript. A high overall GPA built partly on courses that do not qualify can still fall short. Check your high school's approved course list early, not in junior year.

**Amateurism.** Athletes must be certified as amateurs. Anything involving payment, an agent, or playing professionally can affect this. If your family is unsure whether something counts, ask before it happens.

**The school's own admissions bar.** Separate from the NCAA. A coach can want your athlete and admissions can still say no. At academically selective schools, this is the real gate.

NAIA runs its own eligibility center with its own rules, and junior college requirements are different again. Whichever direction your athlete goes, find the requirements at the start rather than the end.

## How the money works

Athletic money in soccer is mostly partial. Coaches divide a limited pool across a roster, so a typical offer is a percentage rather than a full ride, and it is usually stacked with other aid.

That is why grades pay. Academic money, need-based aid and merit awards frequently exceed a partial athletic offer, and at D3 they are the only money on the table. A strong student has more total options and often a cheaper final number than a stronger player with weaker grades.

Run the real cost. Compare what your family pays after everything, not the size of the athletic percentage. The school with the smaller soccer offer is sometimes the cheaper school.

## FAQ

**What are the requirements for a college soccer scholarship?**
An athletic evaluation by the coach, plus academic and eligibility clearance. For NCAA D1 and D2 that means registering with the Eligibility Center, completing approved core courses, and being certified as an amateur, alongside the school's own admissions standards.

**Do I need to register with the NCAA Eligibility Center?**
Yes, for D1 and D2. It is not automatic and no coach does it for you. NAIA has its own separate process.

**Do college soccer scholarships cover everything?**
Usually not. Soccer money is mostly awarded in partial amounts and combined with academic or need-based aid. D3 does not award athletic money at all.

**Can good grades get my athlete more money than soccer?**
Often, yes. Academic and need-based aid frequently exceeds a partial athletic offer, and at D3 it is the only money available.

**What if a coach wants my athlete but admissions says no?**
It happens, especially at selective schools. Coach interest and admission are separate decisions, which is why the transcript matters from freshman year.
