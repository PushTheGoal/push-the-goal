Sixty emails out. Four replies, three of them automated. Two showcases nobody watched. A teammate committed in September and your athlete is checking their phone in a way that has stopped being hopeful.

Then one night they say it out loud. Maybe this is a sign. Maybe I am not good enough for this.

I hear that sentence often enough that I have a standard answer, and athletes raise it on my calls without being asked. It is the most common thing in recruiting that nobody puts in the brochure.

## What the silence is not

It is not a verdict on your athlete.

The reasons coaches do not reply are boring and they are almost never about talent. They filled the position. They are in season and drowning. The recruiting assistant left. They are holding the spot to see who enters the portal in spring. The email got buried under two hundred others.

A coach not replying has told you nothing about your athlete. It only feels like a judgment because your athlete sent something that took courage and got nothing back. And when a coach who was replying goes quiet, here is [how to restart the conversation](/when-college-coaches-stop-responding/).

That is worth saying out loud in your house, more than once.

## The comparison problem

The teammate who committed in September is the hardest part.

Recruiting timelines vary wildly by position, level, and how programs fill their classes. Some athletes commit early because a coach needed that position that year. Others sign in the spring of senior year at a better fit for more money. If your athlete is a junior or senior wondering whether the window has closed, read [is it too late to get recruited](/is-it-too-late-to-get-recruited/).

Committing first is not winning. Nobody asks in five years what month somebody committed.

Your athlete cannot see that at sixteen, when the whole team is congratulating someone else. Which is why they need an adult repeating it.

## Control the controllables

This is my core frame and it is the most practical thing in this post.

Your athlete cannot control whether a coach opens an email, whether a program has room in their class, whether a scout shows up. Every ounce of energy spent on those is wasted, and worse, it is where despair lives.

What they can control, entirely:

Their film and whether it is current. How many emails go out this week. Whether the follow-ups happen. Their grades. Their fitness. Their touch. How they behave when they come off the field.

Every one of those is available today and none of them require anyone else's permission. An athlete working the controllables has a job. An athlete waiting for a reply has only the waiting.

**The best do not get bored of the basics.** I say this to athletes constantly. The greatest players in the world drill the same fundamentals as everyone else and do them better. Recruiting is the same. Film, emails, follow-up, grades, repeat. It is not exciting and it is what works.

## Where momentum comes back

**Follow-ups.** Responses come on the third and fourth attempt. Athletes who stop at two never see the thing that was about to happen. I have watched athletes get two to four replies per round once they got to the later rounds, and the coaches said as much: this is the fourth time you have emailed me.

**Widening the list.** An athlete emailing twelve schools and hearing nothing does not have a talent problem, they have a sample-size problem. Fifty to a hundred schools across levels changes the odds.

**Something new to send.** New film gives a reason to contact everyone again. So does a [personal statement video](/soccer-personal-statement-video/), a good result, a grade improvement.

**A small measurable win.** I assign concrete targets: a number of juggles by the end of summer, posting three times a week, water intake, added running. Not because juggling gets an athlete recruited, but because a kid who is hitting targets feels like someone in motion instead of someone waiting.

## If they want to quit

Listen first. Do not argue immediately, because an athlete who gets argued with stops telling you things.

Then separate two questions that get tangled together. Does your athlete still love playing? And are they exhausted by the recruiting process? Those are different, and the second one is fixable without giving up the first.

Often the answer is a break from recruiting rather than a break from soccer. Two weeks with no emails, no target list, no talking about it. Then back to it.

If your athlete has stopped wanting to play college soccer, that is allowed. Some sixteen-year-olds discover they want a normal college experience, and that is a legitimate outcome. What should not happen is quitting on a bad Tuesday in November because the inbox is empty.

My line to an athlete in that spot: it is not a sign to stop, it is a test. How badly do you want this? Some people have to prove it more than others.

And the version I use when an athlete is at the bottom: all I need you to do is just not give up. That is it. Can you do that?

## The long view

The athletes who end up somewhere are not the most talented ones in every case. They are the ones still emailing in February.

That is a hard truth and a hopeful one, because persistence is available to every family reading this. It costs nothing and it is entirely within your athlete's control.

Keep the film current. Keep the emails going. Keep the grades up. Keep showing up.

## FAQ

**Why is nobody responding to my athlete's recruiting emails?**
Usually because coaches are in season, the position is filled, the email was buried, or they are waiting on the transfer portal. Silence is rarely about your athlete's ability.

**My athlete wants to quit because recruiting is not going well. What do I do?**
Separate loving the sport from being worn out by the process. A two-week break from recruiting fixes more than quitting does. If they have stopped wanting to play in college, that is a legitimate choice. Make it on a calm day, after the break.

**How long does college soccer recruiting usually take?**
It varies by position, level and how programs fill classes. Athletes commit anywhere from early high school to the spring of senior year, and later commitments are often better fits.

**What should my athlete focus on when recruiting stalls?**
The controllables: current film, weekly emails, follow-ups, grades, fitness and touch. None of those need anyone else's permission.

**Does following up more than twice work?**
Yes. Responses commonly arrive on the third and fourth round, after most athletes have stopped sending them.
