Your kid is eleven, or twelve, or thirteen. They said the words "I want to play in college" in the car last week and you have not stopped thinking about it. You are not sure whether you are supposed to be doing something, and you are half afraid that everyone else already is.

Here is the honest answer, in two parts.

College coaches are not recruiting your middle schooler. Anyone telling you otherwise is selling something.

And the athletes who have an easy time when recruiting does start, in high school, are almost never the ones who got serious at fifteen. They are the ones who spent the years before that building a few quiet advantages. That is the part you can start now.

## What recruiting is, and when it starts

Recruiting is a specific thing: a college coach evaluating a player for a roster spot, and a family working to get in front of the right coaches. It runs on a calendar built around high school, and the [family's grade-by-grade version of that calendar](/college-soccer-recruiting-timeline/) begins in ninth grade. The [coach's side of it](/when-does-college-soccer-recruiting-start/) has rules about when they can even contact an athlete.

None of that applies to a sixth grader. There is no highlight video to send, no coach to email, no target list to build. If someone is offering your eleven-year-old a recruiting profile or a showcase package, walk away.

So the answer to "when should my kid start recruiting" is high school. The question underneath it is different: what should they be doing before then so that high school goes well?

## What the athletes who recruit well have in common

I work with a lot of high school athletes at Push The Goal, and after enough of them you notice a pattern. The ones who move fast in recruiting share a few things that have nothing to do with how good they were at twelve.

**They already have the habits.** They do the work when nobody is checking. They show up early, they take care of their bodies, they get their schoolwork done without a fight. Coaches can spot this in a fifteen-year-old within one conversation, and it cannot be installed the summer before freshman year.

**They can talk.** To adults, to coaches, in a full sentence, about their own game. The athletes who freeze on a call with a college coach at sixteen are usually the ones who never had to speak for themselves before.

**They still love it.** This one matters more than any of the others. A lot of the most talented ten-year-olds are out of the game by fourteen. Burnout, pressure, too many seasons, a parent who wanted it more than they did. An athlete who arrives in high school still hungry has already beaten most of the field.

**They know what they are working toward.** Not a specific school. A picture of what college soccer is, what the levels are, what it takes. An athlete who understands the road ahead trains differently from one who is told to.

## What to do in grades 5 through 8

Keep it small. These years are for building the person who will be recruited later, and for keeping the game fun.

**Let them play, a lot, and not only in structured settings.** Pickup, futsal, the backyard. Touches on the ball with no adult correcting them are where creativity comes from. Coaches notice creativity at seventeen and trace it back to here.

**Build one habit at a time.** Water bottles finished. A short set of touches every day. Stretching before bed. One small thing, kept for a month, then another. I ran my own career on a whiteboard in my bedroom with the week's targets on it. The whiteboard matters more than what is written on it.

**Let them own something.** Their bag. Their schedule. Talking to their own coach when they have a question. Every task a parent does for a twelve-year-old is a task a sixteen-year-old cannot do for themselves in front of a college coach.

**Give them someone to answer to who is not you.** This is the one parents underrate. A kid will argue with a parent about training. They will not argue with a coach they respect who asks, every week, "why did we not get it done?" Accountability from outside the house changes how a young athlete works, and it takes the fight out of your kitchen.

**Take the pressure off, on purpose.** No talk of scholarships at the dinner table. No rankings. No comparisons to the kid on the other team. Protect the love of the game like it is the asset it is, because it is.

## What to leave alone until high school

Highlight videos. Recruiting profiles. Emailing coaches. Showcases marketed as exposure. ID camps at colleges. Committing to a position or a "path" at twelve. Anything with the word "elite" attached to a price tag for a fifth grader.

All of it has a place. That place is later, when there is a coach on the other end who can do something with it. Doing it early does not get your athlete ahead. It gets them tired.

## The honest version of "are we behind?"

You are not behind. There is nothing to be behind on yet.

There is something to be ahead on, and it is unglamorous: habits, independence, confidence, and a kid who still wants to play. Families who spend these years on those four things walk into freshman year with a head start they cannot buy later.

## FAQ

**When does college soccer recruiting start?**
In high school. The family's work begins around ninth grade and the coach's calendar has rules about contact. Nothing about recruiting applies to a middle schooler.

**Is 12 too early to think about college soccer?**
Too early to recruit, yes. Too early to build the habits, confidence and independence that make recruiting go well, no. Those are the work of grades 5 through 8.

**Should my middle schooler have a highlight video?**
No. There is no coach who can act on it yet. Videos, profiles and coach outreach belong in high school.

**What should a 5th to 8th grader do to prepare for college soccer?**
Play a lot, including unstructured play. Build small daily habits. Learn to speak for themselves. Answer to a coach outside the family. Keep the game fun.
