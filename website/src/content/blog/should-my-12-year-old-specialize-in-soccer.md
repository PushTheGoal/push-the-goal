The club director says it kindly. If she wants to play in college, she needs to commit. Year-round. Drop basketball. Add the winter league and the spring trainer. Everyone serious is doing it.

It sounds like commitment. In our experience with high school athletes, it is one of the most common reasons a promising player is injured, tired or gone by fifteen. Here is what we tell families who ask.

## What the evidence says

Pediatric sports medicine bodies have recommended for years that young athletes delay single-sport specialization until the mid-teens. The reasons are consistent across the research: kids who specialize early get more overuse injuries, burn out at higher rates, and do not end up better than multi-sport kids at the same sport by the time they are seventeen.

That last part surprises parents. The extra thousand hours at eleven do not produce a better sixteen-year-old. Often they produce a sixteen-year-old with a chronic knee and no love left for the game.

## What college coaches say

Ask a college coach whether they want a recruit who played only soccer from age nine or one who played soccer and two other sports through middle school. Most will take the multi-sport athlete, and they will tell you why: better all-round athleticism, fewer injuries in their history, more coachability because they have learned from more coaches, and a player who chose soccer instead of one who was assigned it.

Coaches also read burnout risk. A recruit who has been going year-round since fourth grade is a recruit who might quit in college. Coaches have seen it.

## Why it happens anyway

**Fear.** The other families are doing it. If you do not, your kid falls behind. This is the pitch, and it works on loving parents.

**Business.** Year-round programs, winter leagues and private trainers are revenue. A club that keeps your athlete twelve months a year is a club with predictable income. That is a reason to check whose interest the advice serves.

**Confusing hours with progress.** More sessions feel like more development. For a growing body, more sessions past a point are more load, and load without rest is how twelve-year-olds end up in physical therapy.

## What a good middle school year looks like

**A real soccer season with an end.** A fall or spring club season, done well, with a break after it.

**A second sport, or unstructured time.** Basketball, track, swimming, anything with different movement. Or nothing organized, and a lot of pickup soccer in the yard. Both count.

**Daily small habits, not daily sessions.** Ten minutes of touches. Juggling. Water. Sleep. These are what build a player in the years before high school, and they cost nothing.

**Someone tracking the whole picture.** Not the club coach, who sees one season. Not the parent, who gets argued with. A coach in the athlete's corner who knows the calendar, the body, the school load and the mood, and who says "this month we rest" with authority a parent does not have at home.

## When specializing starts to make sense

Around the start of high school, when recruiting begins and the athlete has chosen, out loud and on their own, that soccer is the one. Even then, coaches want an off-season. The players Izi works with through recruiting who are still healthy and still hungry at seventeen are almost never the ones who went year-round at eleven.

## The real question underneath

"Should we specialize" is usually "are we doing enough." The answer, for a twelve-year-old, is that enough looks like less than you are being sold. A season, a break, another sport, small daily habits, and a kid who cannot wait for the next season to start. That athlete arrives in high school ahead of the one who played twelve months a year and is already tired.

## FAQ

**Should kids specialize in one sport at 12?**
The consistent recommendation from pediatric sports medicine is to wait until the mid-teens. Early specialization is linked to more overuse injuries and burnout, and it does not produce better players by seventeen.

**Do college soccer coaches prefer multi-sport athletes?**
Most say yes. They cite better athleticism, fewer injuries in the history, more coachability, and a lower risk of burnout in college.

**Will my kid fall behind if they do not play soccer year-round?**
No. The players who stand out at fifteen are the ones who arrive healthy, hungry and with good habits, not the ones with the most hours logged at eleven.

**When should a soccer player start specializing?**
Around the start of high school, when the athlete has chosen soccer for themselves and recruiting begins. Even then, coaches want an off-season every year.

**How much soccer is too much for a 12-year-old?**
A season with an end, a break after it, and small daily habits is plenty. Year-round club plus a winter league plus a private trainer is load without rest, and that is where injuries come from.
