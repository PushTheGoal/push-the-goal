Most recruiting advice is written as if boys and girls run the same race. They do not. The women's game recruits earlier, carries more money, fills rosters differently, and asks different things of a fifteen-year-old and her family.

Izi played Division I women's soccer after two years of junior college and five knee surgeries. This is the women's side of recruiting, from someone who was recruited on it.

## It starts earlier, and that cuts both ways

Women's programs have historically committed players younger than men's programs do. Coaches build classes early, and a strong sophomore can have real conversations going while a boy the same age is still a year from being looked at seriously. The [coach's calendar](/when-does-college-soccer-recruiting-start/) and the [family timeline](/college-soccer-recruiting-timeline/) both matter more on the women's side, because the windows are earlier.

The upside: a prepared family gets clarity sooner. The downside: an unprepared family can feel behind in tenth grade without having done anything wrong. If your daughter is a junior and has not started, she is late by the women's calendar and still recruitable. The late windows exist on the women's side too, and they are less crowded.

## There is more money, and more programs

Title IX means women's soccer carries more scholarship money per program than men's, and a lot of schools sponsor women's soccer that do not field a men's team. More programs and more money means more places for a good player to land funded.

It also means the money is worth understanding. Partial scholarships are the norm, packages get stacked with academic money, and a coach with more to give has more to negotiate with. [Academic money vs athletic money](/academic-money-vs-athletic-money/) applies with extra force here, because a strong student who is also a good player is the best-funded athlete on the women's side.

## Rosters are more domestic

Men's D1 rosters lean heavily on [international players](/international-players-d1-soccer-rosters/). Women's rosters are more American, though that is shifting. For a girl recruiting now, the competition for a spot is still mostly other American club players, which makes exposure and outreach matter more than they do for boys, and makes the club and showcase circuit more relevant.

That does not mean the club name gets her recruited. It means the events where women's coaches are watching are worth planning around, with outreach sent before every one of them so a coach is looking for her, not scanning a field.

## What coaches on the women's side say they want

The same things every coach wants, with two things they mention more often.

**Soccer IQ and communication.** Women's coaches talk about game understanding and on-field communication as separators. Film that shows your daughter organizing, talking, and reading the game does work that a compilation of goals does not.

**Character and culture fit.** Women's programs are close groups and coaches recruit for the room as much as the field. The personal statement video, the way she carries a phone call, how she treats the team manager on a visit: coaches notice. Families sometimes treat this as soft. Coaches treat it as a tiebreaker.

## Injury, and the honest conversation about knees

Female athletes tear ACLs at higher rates than male athletes. Every women's coach knows this, has players who have been through it, and does not treat an injured recruit as damaged goods. Izi came back from five knee surgeries and finished a Division I career. The [recruiting after an injury](/recruiting-after-an-injury/) post covers what to do. The short version: tell coaches early and honestly, keep them updated, and keep doing the work you can do. Coaches respect that more than families expect.

## What this means for your family

**Start the conversation earlier than you would for a son.** Not the pressure. The preparation: film, a first list, an understanding of the calendar, by the start of sophomore year.

**Use the numbers in your favor.** More programs, more money. A wide list on the women's side is a good list.

**Plan exposure around where women's coaches are.** Outreach first, event second, follow-up third.

**Show the whole player.** Communication, game reading, character. Film and outreach that show a person, not a highlight package.

**If you are late, you are not out.** The women's calendar runs early, and it also runs long. Spots and money open every spring.

## FAQ

**Is college soccer recruiting different for girls than boys?**
Yes. The women's side commits earlier, carries more scholarship money per program, has more programs, and fills rosters with more domestic players. Exposure and outreach matter more, and the timeline starts sooner.

**When should a girl start college soccer recruiting?**
Preparation by the start of sophomore year: film, a first list, an understanding of the calendar. Juniors who have not started are late by the women's calendar and still recruitable.

**Are there more soccer scholarships for women than men?**
Per program, yes, because of Title IX, and more schools sponsor women's soccer than men's. Partial scholarships stacked with academic money are the norm.

**Does an ACL injury end a girl's recruiting?**
No. Women's coaches see it constantly and recruit through it. Tell them early, keep them updated, and keep working. Izi finished a D1 career after five knee surgeries.

**What do women's college soccer coaches look for most?**
The same fundamentals every coach wants, plus two they name more often: game understanding with on-field communication, and character that fits a close team.
