Every field player on your athlete's team is competing for one of several spots in their position group. Your keeper is competing for one.

That single fact changes the entire process. Fewer openings, a longer timeline, different film, and a different conversation with coaches. Most keeper families run the field-player playbook and wonder why it is slower.

There is an upside to that, and it is a big one. Goalkeeper is arguably the easiest position to market, because it is close to a different sport. A field player's film has to argue that they are better than thousands of players doing the same things. A keeper's film shows a specialist skill set that most viewers cannot evaluate against a crowd, and high-level keeper training with genuine saves in it is immediately, obviously impressive.

That is a marketing advantage. Use it. A keeper with sharp, well-shot technical footage stands out faster than a forward with the same production value, because there is less of it out there.

## Why the math is different

A college program carries a small number of keepers, and they rarely all graduate at once. A school might recruit a keeper one year and then not need another for two or three.

The practical effect: your keeper's list has to be wider than a field player's, and timing matters more. A program that loves your athlete may have no room in their class, which is not a rejection of ability. Ask directly whether they are taking a keeper in that year, early, because it saves everyone months.

## What college coaches want from a keeper

**Distribution.** This is the biggest change in keeper recruiting and the thing most high school keepers neglect. Coaches want to see your athlete start attacks: throwing to feet, playing out under pressure, hitting a switch. A reel of diving saves and nothing else tells a coach your keeper cannot be part of the build.

**Decision making off the line.** Coming for crosses, sweeping behind a high line, choosing when to stay. Coaches watch judgment more than reflexes.

**Handling and footwork under pressure.** Clean technique repeated, rather than one spectacular moment.

**Organization and voice.** A keeper who talks is running the defense. This shows on film even without audio, in how the back line adjusts.

**Composure after conceding.** Every keeper gets scored on. Coaches watch the next five minutes closely, because a keeper's reaction sets the tone for the whole team.

## The keeper's film is a different film

Do not send a field player's highlight reel with saves in it.

**Show variety, not only saves.** Distribution, claims, sweeping, one-v-ones, organization, footwork. A coach wants to know what kind of keeper your athlete is.

**Include the whole sequence.** Start the clip before the save so a coach sees positioning and reading, which is what they are judging. A save that starts at the dive tells them nothing about why your keeper was in the right place.

**Mark yourself early.** A spotlight or arrow in the opening seconds. Obvious for a keeper, and still worth doing.

**Include training clips.** Keeper-specific work is genuinely useful for a coach evaluating technique, more so than for field players.

**Keep it to three or four minutes**, strongest first. The rules in our [recruiting video guide](/how-to-make-a-soccer-recruiting-video/) all still apply.

## Getting seen

Keepers benefit from ID camps more than most positions. In a camp your athlete gets hands-on evaluation from the staff, often including a dedicated keeper coach, and hours of technical work a showcase cannot show. If budget allows one thing, a camp at a target school usually beats another tournament.

At showcases, tell coaches specifically that your athlete is a keeper and which half they are playing. Keepers rotate, and a coach who arrives during the wrong half sees nothing. That one line in a pre-event email is the difference between being watched and being missed.

## A word to keeper families

The position tests patience in ways the others do not. Fewer spots, slower interest, and a season where your athlete can play well and still lose.

Controlling the controllables is the whole game here. Distribution work, footwork, film, emails, follow-ups. The best keepers do not get bored of the basics. They do the basics better than everyone else, and college coaches can see it.

## FAQ

**Is goalkeeper recruiting harder than field player recruiting?**
Different rather than harder. There are fewer roster spots per class, so the target list needs to be wider and timing matters more, but there is also less competition for a coach's attention when a program does need a keeper.

**What should a goalkeeper put in a recruiting video?**
Distribution, claims, sweeping, one-v-ones, organization and footwork alongside saves, with each clip starting early enough to show positioning. Three to four minutes, strongest first.

**Do college coaches want keepers who play with their feet?**
Yes. Distribution is one of the first things evaluated, and it is what most high school keeper reels are missing.

**Are ID camps worth it for goalkeepers?**
Often more than for field players, because keepers get dedicated technical evaluation that a showcase cannot provide.

**How do I make sure a coach sees my keeper at a showcase?**
Email beforehand with the team, field, times and which half your athlete is playing. Keepers rotate, and coaches who arrive at the wrong time see nothing.
