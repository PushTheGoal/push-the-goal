"No coaches are responding."

Every week I hear this from a frustrated parent, and I understand why it stings. Your athlete worked up the courage to email a college coach, hit send on something that felt important, and got silence back. Do that five times and it starts to feel personal.

It is almost never personal. College coaches get hundreds of recruiting emails. Most get skimmed for five seconds or never opened at all. The difference between the emails that get responses and the ones that disappear is not talent. It is knowing what a coach looks for in those five seconds, and having a follow-up system for everything else.

Here is the whole playbook.

## First rule: the athlete sends the email

Not you. I know you are reading this because you handle the logistics in your family, and there is plenty for you to help with behind the scenes. But the email comes from your athlete's account, in your athlete's words.

Coaches are not just recruiting a player. They are recruiting a young adult they will coach, push, and live alongside for four years. An email from mom or dad tells the coach who is driving this process, and it is the wrong answer. Parents build the list, proofread, and manage the calendar. Athletes hit send.

## What a great recruiting email looks like

Short. Specific. Impossible to mistake for a mass blast. Five parts:

**1. A subject line that does the coach's filing for them.** Graduation year, position, name, one hook. "2027 Center Back · Maria Lopez · 3.8 GPA · Film Inside" beats "Recruiting Interest" every time.

Always put an emoji in the subject line. I ask every athlete I coach to do this. A coach scrolling a wall of gray text stops on the one line with a soccer ball or a lightning bolt in it, and that half-second of attention is the entire job of a subject line. One emoji, at the front or the back. Keep the rest of it clean.

**2. One line that proves it is not a template.** Reference something true about their program: their style of play, a result, what the school offers academically. One honest sentence. Coaches smell find-and-replace from a mile away.

**3. The vital stats, fast.** Graduation year, position, club team, jersey number, GPA, city and state. Two lines, not two paragraphs.

**4. The film link, visible and working.** The highlight video is the whole reason this email exists. Put the link where a thumb can hit it and make sure it opens without a login.

**5. What is next.** Upcoming tournament or showcase schedule with dates and field info, plus one clear closing question, like "Would you like me to send my schedule when brackets are posted?" A question gives a coach a reason to reply.

That is it. No life story, no letter of recommendation, no ten attached photos. Under 200 words, every word working.

## When to send it (timing matters more than families think)

Two timing rules. First, remember the contact rule from our [recruiting timeline guide](/college-soccer-recruiting-timeline/): NCAA D1 and D2 coaches cannot respond directly until June 15 after your athlete's sophomore year. Emails sent before then are not wasted, coaches read and file them, but do not expect a reply, because the rules forbid it.

Second, send when coaches are looking. Early mornings midweek beat Friday nights. The weeks before a big showcase beat the middle of the college season, when a coach's attention is on their own team. And any time your athlete has fresh film or new results, that is a reason to hit send.

## The follow-up system (this is where recruiting is won)

One email is an introduction. The follow-up is the recruitment. Here is the rhythm I teach:

- **Week 0:** the intro email.
- **1 to 2 weeks later:** a short update. New film clip, recent results, or the showcase schedule. Two sentences and a link. Families wait too long here, and a month of silence lets your athlete slide out of a coach's memory.
- **Before every event a target coach might attend:** date, time, field, jersey number. Make it effortless to watch your athlete.
- **After the event:** one line. "We went 2-1 this weekend, my assist at 1:14 of this clip came from Saturday's game."
- **Repeat, every week or two, with something real each time.**

Persistence is not pestering when every message adds something new. Coaches read consistency as maturity, and maturity as coachability.

## Why coaches go silent, honestly

Sometimes silence means not interested, and no amount of emailing changes that. But just as often it means: not allowed to reply yet, buried inbox, waiting to see the athlete live, or waiting to see if the athlete follows up. You cannot control which one it is. You can control whether your athlete disappears after one attempt. Most do. Yours should not.

And if a coach says no, thank them and move on fast. A quick no is a gift. It points your energy at the programs still in play.

## FAQ

**Should parents email college coaches?**
No. Coaches want to hear from the athlete. Parents help best behind the scenes: building the school list, proofreading, and keeping the follow-up calendar on track.

**How long should a recruiting email be?**
Under 200 words. Subject line with grad year, position, and an emoji, one personal line about the program, key stats, film link, upcoming schedule, one closing question.

**How often should my athlete follow up with a coach?**
Every one to two weeks, and always with something new: fresh film, results, or an upcoming schedule. Consistent, substantive follow-up is what separates recruited athletes from overlooked ones.

**My sophomore emailed a D1 coach and heard nothing back. Why?**
NCAA rules prevent D1 and D2 coaches from replying until June 15 after sophomore year. Keep the updates coming anyway. Coaches read and file them, and plenty reach out the moment that date passes.
