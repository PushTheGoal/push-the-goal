"Our club coach said he'd make some calls."

I hear that sentence a lot, usually from a family who has been waiting on those calls for eight months. This post is not about bad club coaches. Most of them are good people working hard. It is about what the job structurally is, and what it is not, so your family stops waiting for something that was never coming.

## What a good club coach genuinely does

**Develops your athlete.** The actual job, and the thing that matters most over four years.

**Puts them in front of competition.** Choosing the right events and the right level of play is real recruiting value.

**Provides an honest evaluation.** A coach willing to tell you your athlete is a D3 player rather than a D1 player is doing you an enormous favor, even when it does not feel like one.

**Confirms character when asked.** If a college coach calls to ask what your kid is like in a locker room, your club coach's answer carries weight. Note the direction of that call: the college coach initiates it, usually late, after they are already interested.

## What they will not do

**Run your athlete's outreach.** No club coach has time to email fifty college programs for every player on the roster, and multiply that across several teams. It is not a lack of care. It is arithmetic.

**Advocate equally for everyone.** Every roster has a player the coach pushes for. If it is not yours, no amount of hoping changes it, and you will never be told.

**Guarantee anyone sees your kid.** This is the big one, and it has changed.

## The part nobody warns you about: the information goes stale

Recruiting rules and norms change constantly. Contact dates get revised, transfer rules move, roster limits shift, and what worked for a family two years ago can be wrong today. I have to stay updated on it as part of my job, and I am talking to college coaches all the time to do it. It gets harder every year, not easier.

A club coach who is not in the recruiting process day to day has no realistic way to keep up with that. This is not a knock on them. They are coaching, running sessions, managing rosters and traveling, and the recruiting landscape they learned is the one from whenever they last went through it.

So the advice you get can be sincere, confident and out of date at the same time. That combination is the dangerous one, because nothing about it sounds wrong.

Take the development advice, and verify anything about rules, timing or process against a current source before you build a plan on it.

## The part that has changed

Relying on a club coach's college contacts used to be a real strategy. It carries much less weight now than it did a few years ago.

And a college coach traveling to one standalone club game is rare. It happens, and when it does, it comes after months of relationship building: emails, film, camp visits, real conversations. No family should build a plan around a college coach showing up to a Saturday match because someone made a call.

Build it around showcases, camps and film, where coaches are already going to be, and around your athlete's own outreach, which is what puts their name on the list a coach carries into those events.

## How to use your club coach well

**Tell them your list.** Give them the schools your athlete is targeting. If a genuine connection exists at one of them, this is how it surfaces. Do not make them guess.

**Ask for the honest level read, and hear it.** Ask directly: where do you see this player at the next level. Then believe the answer, even if it is lower than you hoped. That information is worth more than an introduction.

**Ask for availability, not advocacy.** Would you take a call from a college coach about my athlete. Almost every club coach says yes, and that is the useful commitment.

**Ask about minutes.** Where your athlete plays and how much shapes their film for the whole season. That conversation is more valuable than any contact list.

**Do not ask them to do the emailing.** They will not, and the asking strains a relationship you need.

## Who owns this

Nobody advocates for your athlete like your athlete.

The division of labor that works: your athlete owns the film, the social presence and the emails. Your family owns the target list, the calendar and the proofreading. The club coach owns development and gets told where you are aiming. Nobody in that arrangement is waiting on anybody else.

An athlete who runs their own recruiting also arrives at college ready to advocate for themselves with a coach who has ninety-nine other things happening, which is the actual skill this whole process is teaching.

## FAQ

**Will my club coach get my athlete recruited?**
No. A good club coach develops your athlete, chooses strong competition, gives an honest level read and vouches for character when a college coach calls. The outreach has to come from your athlete.

**Do college coaches still recruit through club coach referrals?**
Far less than they used to. Referrals can support an existing conversation, but they rarely start one, so treat a referral as a bonus rather than a plan.

**Will a college coach come to my athlete's club game?**
Rarely. It happens after months of relationship building. Coaches concentrate their travel on showcases, tournaments and camps where many recruits play at once.

**What should I ask my club coach?**
Where they see your athlete playing at the next level, whether they will take a call from a college coach, and what your athlete needs to do to earn more minutes.

**Is it rude to recruit without involving the club coach?**
No. Tell them your target list and keep them informed. Running your own outreach is expected and reads well to college coaches.

**Is my club coach's recruiting advice up to date?**
Possibly not, through no fault of theirs. Recruiting rules and norms change constantly, and a coach who is not in the process day to day has little chance of keeping current. Take the development advice and verify anything about rules or timing against a current source.
